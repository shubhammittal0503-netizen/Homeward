For judges

The brief's seven questions, answered in its own words with links to where each is shown; what was deliberately left out; what is measured; what is real. The one-page note, the video and the AI build log are the submission; this page is the map of the prototype.

The problem in one paragraph

Small Indian services firms (agencies, SaaS firms, small IT shops) earn in dollars but cannot always touch the money. Foreign cards get declined and nobody tells the client another way to pay; transfers arrive and sit uncaptured until an invoice is matched by hand; from 1 October 2026 every services invoice needs an export declaration, and some banks need the form's number before the money can be captured at all. Razorpay's Exporter Dashboard shows these states and its invoice-upload and pending-action flows ask the merchant to act; nothing today works out the fix across declines, matching and export forms and prepares it for one approval. Homeward treats the invoice as the unit of work and, each morning, lists the money that is not home, says why, and prepares the fix for the owner to approve. In the demo morning that is ₹24.6 lakh across 11 items (synthetic).

The seven questions

  1. What data or signal identifies the opportunity

    Razorpay's own payment states: card declines with their reason text; cards authorised but not captured, with the 3-day refund clock; MoneySaver transfers waiting for an invoice or buyer details; review holds; replies to the agent's notes. Plus the merchant's invoices (Zoho, read-only) and the rules that decide which invoices need an export form and when.

    a decline with its reason · a transfer waiting for an invoice · invoices that need an EDF now

  2. Why AI is necessary and what it does better than a fixed rule

    Rules first: deadlines, limits, exemptions, exact matches and route selection are rules. The model reads what needs judgement, which the fair rules only partly cover: free-text decline reasons, client replies in other languages (Dutch, Spanish and German in the tests) with negation and sarcasm, messy payer names and references, review requests and invoices, and it drafts. We tested this honestly: a fair rule set written from the policy matches the agent on all 190 published cases, because those cases are structured; on a fresh set written without the keys the agent disagreed with the key once and the fair rules twice. The margin on our own cases is small; the margin on real email and bank narrations is what the pilot measures.

    Test results, with every disagreement · Rules and AI

  3. What recommendation or action the product produces

    One prepared action per item: an invoice upload, a split across invoices, a note to the client with another way to pay, a document pack for a review, an EDF data pack for the bank, a letter to the bank, or a question with equal-weight answers.

    Today's list · a reply read and a wire linked

  4. What the merchant can review, change, approve or stop

    Review-first on by default; Approve, Edit draft, Not now and Don't suggest this on every prepared item; three equal answers on questions; limits set in Controls (reminders per decline, client hours and holidays, the 0.80 threshold, do-not-contact, read-only connections); pause; an audit log of every action and who approved it.

    Controls · the agent stopped itself after a dispute

  5. How the system learns from the outcome

    Three signals (outcomes, edits, answers) change only the choice within what the rules allow: which allowed way to pay to offer first, the wording of drafts, the lean on an ambiguous match, remembered payer names. A preference needs five outcomes or one confirmation before it is used; it never changes clocks, limits, stop rules or the threshold; everything learned is visible and undoable. Policy section 11.

    Learning · a payer name learned on approval

  6. How accuracy, permissions, trust and failure cases are handled

    Blind tests against two rule baselines with every disagreement published; confidence shown only on AI steps and the agent asks below 0.80; read-only access to the billing tool and one Drive folder; the model never sees card numbers or statements; client messages and references are data, never instructions (a 10-case safety set); rules-only mode when the AI is down; a wrong-link recovery path; the EDF is the exporter's declaration and the agent never files with a bank.

    Test results · Rules-only mode · When things go wrong

  7. What business outcome would be measured

    Primary: rupees recovered per merchant per month (declined cards won back), against a holdout. Second line: rupees released from holds, and days from payment attempt to money in the bank. Safety: wrong-match rate, client complaints or opt-outs, draft rejections. For Razorpay: international volume kept per exporter, exporter churn, support tickets per exporter. Next test: a pilot of 1,000 Razorpay exporters for 8 weeks, half with the agent and half without, which must also disprove that a checklist gets the same result.

What was deliberately left out, and why

  • Disputes and chargebacksRazorpay already has the Dispute Responder and chargeback protection; the agent stops and hands over.
  • Currency timingXflow already sells FX timing; it is not recovery.
  • Goods exportersTheir EDF goes through customs with the shipping bill.
  • Payouts from Amazon, Upwork and FreelancerRazorpay does not ask for invoices for them.
  • Filing with the bankThe bank is the authority; the agent prepares, the owner submits.
  • Sending without approvalTrust, and Agent Studio's own principle that no irreversible action happens without explicit approval.
  • General unpaid-invoice remindersRazorpayX's Receivables Agent already chases unpaid invoices; Homeward acts only when a payment has failed or money is stuck.
  • Large firms with treasury teamsDifferent needs; small teams feel the pain most.
  • A live integration in the prototypeThree weeks went on a rulebook, a blind test and a static site every reader can open, rather than a brittle Zoho or email connection; the judgement, not the plumbing, is what this entry shows.

Why now, and why Razorpay

  • The EDF regime began on 1 October 2026 and the regulation was amended on 22 September; Razorpay's own help for it is "reach out to our cross-border support team".
  • Razorpay's cross-border business grows 40% a year and was loss-making in October 2025; rivals sell "we don't hold your money" and EDF tools.
  • Agent Studio has no cross-border agent; Exporter Dashboard 2.0 tracks but does not act; the Receivables Agent chases unpaid invoices, which Homeward leaves to it.
  • The guardrails here are Agent Studio's published principles, line by line: approval before irreversible action, the merchant's ceiling, a no is a no, a full audit trail, review-first, no dark patterns.

Assumptions to validate with Razorpay data in the pilot

  • Decline rates and reasons on exporter accounts
  • Share and length of payments waiting for documents, and how many end in automatic refunds
  • Share of MoneySaver users whose bank needs an EDF number before capture
  • How often a client pays another way after a decline, and the recovery rate by route
  • How many drafts the owner approves, edits or rejects
  • Real bank narrations and email threads: where the model's margin over rules actually is

What is real, made up, not built and not tested

  • Real: the rules and their sources (re-read 12 October 2026), Razorpay's products and list fees, the public merchant quotes, the test method and its results on the published sets.
  • Made up: the merchant, clients, invoices, emails, amounts and the learning history.
  • Not built: connections to Razorpay, banks, Zoho or email; live AI calls; the AI outputs shown were produced during the build and stored.
  • Not tested: real bank feeds, real email, real decline text; no merchant was interviewed.
